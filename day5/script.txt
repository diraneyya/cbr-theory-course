Welcome to day five. The first four days were about the world outside your windscreen — priority, signs, speed, other people. Today the exam turns the camera around and asks about you: what is in your blood, what is in your hand, what is in your glovebox. These questions are the cheapest points on the whole exam — pure facts, no judgement calls — as long as you know the Dutch numbers. So let us collect them.

1. Alcohol — and why the strict limit is yours.

The Dutch legal limit for an experienced driver is zero point five promille of alcohol in the blood. But there is a second, stricter limit, and it is the one that matters to you: for a novice driver — anyone in the first five years of holding their licence — the limit is zero point two promille. And here is the detail that catches experienced foreigners: novice means the first five years of this licence. When your Dutch licence is issued, the clock starts, and your years of driving abroad do not count. Zero point two is less than a single glass — in practice it means: if you drive, you drink nothing. You do not drink anyway — which means on this topic you are, for once, the ideal Dutch driver. But the exam will still ask you for both numbers, so hold them: zero point five normal, zero point two novice. Refusing the breath test, by the way, is itself an offence — treated as seriously as failing it.

2. Drugs, medicines, and being tired.

For illegal drugs the law is simpler still: zero tolerance. Since 2017 the police test by saliva at the roadside, and for cannabis, cocaine, XTC, amphetamines and the rest, any measurable amount is an offence — there is no "small enough" the way there is with alcohol, and combinations are punished harder still. Medicines are the subtler exam question. Plenty of ordinary prescriptions — sleeping pills, strong painkillers, some antihistamines — impair driving, and Dutch pharmacies mark those boxes with a yellow warning sticker: this medicine can affect your reaction time. The sticker is your signal to ask the pharmacist whether you may drive; with some medicines you simply may not. And fatigue belongs in this family too: tiredness impairs you the way alcohol does, and the only correct answer the exam will ever accept about driving tired is — recognise it, stop, and rest. Not coffee, not open windows, not louder music.

3. The paperwork — four things in order.

Four pieces of paper make a drive legal before the engine even starts. Your driving licence — the [nl: rijbewijs] — which you must carry and show to police on demand. The registration certificate — the [nl: kentekenbewijs] — proving the car is registered and to whom. Third-party liability insurance — [nl: WA-verzekering], for [nl: wettelijke aansprakelijkheid] — which is mandatory for every car on the public road: it covers the damage you do to others, and driving uninsured is a serious offence even if you never crash. And the periodic technical inspection — the [nl: APK] — which must be valid; an expired inspection, like missing insurance, means the car does not belong on the public road at all. Beyond the mandatory [nl: WA], you may insure the car itself — partial or all-risk cover — but that is your choice; the exam's interest is the mandatory layer. One habit ties this together: these are conditions, not formalities. The exam phrases it as "may this driver drive this car?" — and the answer lives in these four boxes.

4. The phone — a rule about hands.

The mobile phone rule is mercifully precise: while driving you may not hold a mobile device. Not to call, not to navigate, not for two seconds at a red light — because waiting in traffic still counts as driving. The offence is in the hand, not in the phone: mounted in a cradle and operated hands-free, the same phone is legal. So the two exam answers to lock in: at a red light with the engine running, touching the phone in your hand is an offence; the same action with the phone in a holder is not. Parked, out of traffic, engine off — then you are no longer a driver, and the rule lets go of you. The fine, for what it is worth, is one of the heaviest of the common offences — the Netherlands treats a phone in the hand as seriously as serious speeding.

5. Belts and children — the metre thirty-five line.

Seatbelts: everyone, every seat, every trip. No exception for the back seat, none for short trips. For children the law draws a line at one metre thirty-five: a child shorter than that must ride in an approved child seat or booster — an ordinary belt alone is not enough — and a child taller than the line wears the normal belt. And who pays if a child is loose in the car? You do: the driver is legally responsible for passengers under twelve. An adult passenger who refuses their belt collects their own fine — but the children are on you.

6. Practice — five exam-style questions.

My own questions in the exam's style — drill the reasoning. Pause, decide, then listen.

Question one. A driver has held their licence for three years and has drunk one glass of beer, bringing them to roughly zero point three promille. May they drive?

No. Three years into the licence they are still a novice — the novice limit of zero point two applies for the first five years — and zero point three is over it. The same blood value would be legal for a driver of ten years. The exam is testing whether you attach the right limit to the right driver.

Question two. Your pharmacy hands you a prescription medicine with a yellow warning sticker on the box. What does the sticker mean for driving?

It means the medicine can affect your reaction time and driving ability — and that you must find out, from the pharmacist or the leaflet, whether driving is allowed with it. It is not an automatic ban, but it is never ignorable: with some of these medicines driving is genuinely forbidden. "The doctor prescribed it, so it must be fine to drive" is the trap answer.

Question three. You are stationary at a red traffic light, engine running, and you answer a text with the phone in your hand. Offence or not?

An offence. Waiting at a light is still driving, and the ban is on holding the device. Had the phone been in a cradle and the reply dictated hands-free, it would have been legal. The stationary-at-red scenario is the single most asked phone question — because everybody gets it wrong on the road.

Question four. You borrow a friend's car. It is insured, but its [nl: APK] inspection expired last month. May you drive it to the garage to have it inspected?

No — with an expired inspection the car may not be on the public road, and "I was on my way to fix it" is not a defence the law offers you. There is one narrow exception: driving to a booked inspection appointment. Anything else waits for a trailer or the inspection at home. Note also that the fine would land on you, the driver — checking the paperwork of a borrowed car is your job before you turn the key.

Question five. A seven-year-old child, one metre twenty tall, sits in the back wearing the normal seatbelt. Who, if anyone, is committing an offence?

The driver. Under one metre thirty-five the child needs an approved child seat or booster — the belt alone is not lawful — and for passengers under twelve the responsibility is the driver's, not the child's and not the parents' in the abstract. Height decides the seat; age decides whose fine it is.

Closing.

That is the driver's own rulebook. Zero point five promille — but zero point two for you, and really just zero. Drugs: any amount is too much. A yellow sticker means ask before you drive; tired means stop. Paperwork: licence, registration, mandatory [nl: WA] insurance, valid [nl: APK] — four boxes, all ticked, before any drive is legal. Phone: never in your hand, red lights included. Belts for everyone, a proper seat below one metre thirty-five, and the children are your responsibility.

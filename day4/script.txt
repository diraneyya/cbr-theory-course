Welcome to day four. Days one to three were about you: your priority, your signs, your speed. Today is about everyone else — and in the Netherlands, everyone else is the hard part. This country has more bicycles than people, mopeds in two flavours, trams through the middle of town, and trucks that cannot see you. One principle organises all of it, and the exam writes questions straight from it: the more fragile a road user is, the more the rules protect them. Pedestrians and cyclists get the strongest protection; you, in your steel box, carry the responsibility. Here is today's cast — let us meet them one at a time.

1. Cyclists — the rule that catches every foreign driver.

You know this sign from day two: blue circle, a command — a mandatory cycle path, the [nl: fietspad]. For the cyclist it means "you must ride here"; for you it means bikes are running parallel to your road, often with their own little road, their own crossings, sometimes their own traffic lights. Besides the separate path there is also the [nl: fietsstrook] — a cycle lane painted on your own carriageway, marked with bicycle symbols. Treat it as theirs: you may not park on it, and you may not stop on it.

Now the rule that catches almost every driver trained abroad. You are turning right at an intersection. A cyclist on the cycle path beside you is going straight on. Who goes first? The cyclist — always. The rule from day one applies to bikes exactly as to cars: traffic going straight on the same road goes before traffic that is turning. The cycle path counts as part of the same road. So a right turn in the Netherlands means one extra reflex: mirror, shoulder, cycle path — then turn. The exam will hand you this exact picture, and the wrong answer — "the car is on the road, so the car goes first" — is sitting right there in the options waiting for you.

And when cyclists cross your road somewhere unexpected, this day-two triangle warns you: crossing cyclists ahead. A warning commands nothing — but after day one you know what usually comes with a cycle crossing: shark's teeth on somebody's tarmac deciding who yields. One more cyclist fact worth money on the exam: on many roundabouts inside the built-up area, the cycle path around the roundabout has priority — you leave the roundabout across their circle, and you give way as you do.

2. Pedestrians — the zebra is theirs.

This blue square marks the zebra crossing — the [nl: zebrapad]. The rule is stronger than most countries make it: a pedestrian who is crossing or visibly about to cross has priority. Not "once they are in front of your bumper" — the moment they are clearly stepping toward the stripes, you must be able to stop, which means you approach any zebra at a speed that lets you. Two refinements the exam loves. A person pushing their bicycle across the zebra is a pedestrian and has that priority. A person riding their bicycle across it does not — the zebra protects walkers, not riders. And a blind person signalling with a white cane must be given way, zebra or no zebra, always.

3. Mopeds — two classes, read the number plate.

The Netherlands splits mopeds into two legal classes, and the exam expects you to tell them apart at a glance — by the number plate. A blue plate is a [nl: snorfiets]: limited to twenty-five kilometres per hour, and it rides on the cycle path, among the bicycles. Since 2025 its rider must wear a helmet too. A yellow plate is a [nl: bromfiets]: up to forty-five kilometres per hour, helmet always — and here is the part foreign drivers miss: inside the built-up area the [nl: bromfiets] rides on the road, with the cars. With you. So a forty-five kilometre moped in front of you in town is exactly where it belongs; do not expect it to move over onto the cycle path — since the nineties, that has not been its place.

Outside the built-up area the picture changes, and this sign shows it: a blue circle with both a moped and a bicycle is the combined path — [nl: fiets] and [nl: bromfiets] together. Where you see it, forty-five kilometre mopeds are legitimately on the path. The habit to build: read the plate, know the speed, know where they belong.

4. Trams and buses — heavy, on rails, and favoured.

This triangle warns of a tram crossing. But the tram fact that wins exam points came up on day one, and today it gets its full form: at an equal intersection — no signs, no lights — the tram has priority over you regardless of direction. Right-before-left never applies to a tram; it beats you even coming from your left. A tram cannot swerve and cannot stop quickly; the law simply moves it to the front of the queue. Two more public-transport rules complete the set. At a tram stop where passengers step off into the road, they must be given the chance to reach the pavement — you stop and wait, full stop. And inside the built-up area, when a bus at a bus stop signals that it wants to pull out, you must give it the opportunity — the bus pulling away from its stop has, in town, a small priority of its own.

5. Trucks — the blind spot, and where not to be.

Last, the biggest thing on the road, and the one interaction where you are the fragile party. A truck driver sits high and sees remarkably little: almost nothing directly in front of the cab, a narrow strip on the left — and the killer zone, the entire right side, plus everything directly behind. This is the [nl: dode hoek], the dead angle. The test is beautifully simple: if you cannot see the driver's face in his mirror, he cannot see you. Two consequences for you as a driver. Never linger beside a truck's right flank, and at a traffic light, do not creep up along its right side — if it turns right, it turns through the space you are sitting in. And expect a turning truck to do something strange: swing out left before turning right. It needs the room; do not dive into the gap it opens.

6. Practice — five exam-style questions.

My own questions in the exam's style — drill the reasoning. Pause, decide, then listen.

Question one. You are turning right into a side street. On the cycle path to your right, a cyclist continues straight ahead. Who goes first?

The cyclist. Same road, and straight-through goes before turning — the day-one rule, and the bicycle gets its full benefit. Your indicator gives you no rights; it only announces the manoeuvre you must complete safely, after the cyclist has passed.

Question two. At a zebra crossing, one person is pushing their bicycle across, and another is riding theirs across. Which of them has priority over you?

The one pushing. Walking with the bike makes them a pedestrian, and pedestrians on or clearly about to cross the zebra have priority. The one riding is a cyclist, and the zebra gives cyclists nothing — though "no priority" never means "may be hit"; you still drive carefully.

Question three. In the built-up area, a moped with a blue number plate is ahead. Where does it belong, and how fast can it legally go?

Blue plate is a [nl: snorfiets]: it belongs on the cycle path and is limited to twenty-five kilometres per hour. If it had a yellow plate it would be a [nl: bromfiets] — forty-five, and riding on the road with you, quite legally. The plate colour is the whole answer.

Question four. At an unmarked equal intersection, a tram approaches from your left. Day one taught you that traffic from the left yields to you. Does the tram?

No — you yield. The tram beats right-before-left; at equal intersections a tram has priority whatever direction it comes from. Rails override the compass. This is a favourite exam inversion: they show you the tram on the left precisely because the normal rule points the other way.

Question five. At a red light a truck waits, indicating right. There is room to filter up along its right side to the stop line. Do you take it?

No. That space is the heart of the [nl: dode hoek] — the driver cannot see you there, and when the light turns green the truck's right turn sweeps exactly through it. Stay behind, in your lane, where his mirrors can find you. On the exam, any option that places you beside a truck's right flank is the wrong one.

Closing.

That is the cast. Cyclists: straight-through beats your turn, and their lane is theirs. Pedestrians: the zebra belongs to them from the moment they clearly want to cross. Mopeds: blue plate slow on the path, yellow plate fast on the road. Trams: first at every equal crossing, from any direction — and let the bus pull out. Trucks: stay out of the dead angle, and never sit on their right at a light. One principle ties it together — the more fragile, the more protected; and next to a truck, the fragile one is you.

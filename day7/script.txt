Welcome to day seven — the last lesson. You now hold all the rules; today is about the exam itself. Since April 2025 there is no separate hazard-perception block: it is one continuous set of fifty questions, forty-four correct to pass, in about half an hour. Knowledge and insight are blended, which means some questions ask what the law says, and others ask what a thinking driver does — and telling those two apart is a skill of its own. That skill has two halves: reading the question precisely, and reading the street the way the exam does. Let us do both, and then sweep back through the whole week.

1. Reading the question — three different verbs.

Look again at the bottom of this card, because the cheapest mistakes on the exam are not knowledge mistakes — they are reading mistakes. "May you" asks about the law: is it legal? "Is it wise" asks about safety: legal or not, is it smart? "Must you" asks about obligation: are you required? The same situation can answer differently to each verb. You may drive at eighty on that empty road — but in fog it is not wise. You may enter the intersection — but you must not if you cannot clear it. Answer the verb that was asked, not the one in your head. And when two options both feel defensible, the exam has a consistent personality: the more careful answer is the right one. Not the timid answer — the anticipating one. Speed writes the cheque for every mistake, so in doubt, the option that reduces speed or increases distance wins.

2. Reading the street — the "what could happen" game.

Insight questions stage a street and ask what you do next. The trick is that the answer is never about what is happening — it is about what could. Four classics cover most of them, and they are all in this scene. A ball rolls into the road: a ball never crosses alone — a child is behind it, and the answer is brake hard now, not "steer around the ball". A parked car with someone at the wheel: that door may open, so you pass with a door's width of space, slower. A bus at a stop: pedestrians cross in front of it, hidden from you until they step out — so approaching a stopped bus means easing off and watching its nose. A side road on the right: day one lives forever — an unmarked junction means whoever appears from the right has priority, so your speed must make yielding possible. The pattern behind all four: identify who is hidden or vulnerable, assume they act, and have already chosen your response. When an exam option starts with "reduce speed and…", it is very often the answer.

3. The week in seven minutes — the full recap.

Now the sweep. Day one, priority. Signs and lights rule first; where the road is silent, right before left at equal junctions; shark's teeth — [nl: haaientanden] — mean give way; on the roundabout, the traffic already on it; and trams and emergency vehicles step outside the ladder. Turning traffic always lets straight-through traffic pass, and leaving an exit or an [nl: erf] puts you last in line.

Day two, signs. Read the shape and colour before the symbol: red triangle warns, red circle forbids, blue circle commands, blue square informs. On the tarmac, a solid line is a wall and a dashed line is a door. Classify first, then read — you never memorised a catalogue.

Day three, speed. No sign means the road type answers: fifty in town, eighty outside, one hundred on the [nl: autoweg], one-thirty on the [nl: autosnelweg] — but one hundred by day, six to seven. Zones hold until their end sign. The end of a limit means back to the ladder. Blue squares only advise. And when road, sign and vehicle disagree, the lowest number wins — with a trailer, ninety.

Day four, the others. Cyclists going straight beat your turn, every time. The zebra belongs to pedestrians from the moment they clearly want it — including anyone pushing a bike. Blue plate mopeds ride the cycle path at twenty-five; yellow plates ride the road at forty-five. The tram wins every equal crossing from any direction, and the bus gets its gap when it signals out of a stop. And beside a truck, you are the fragile one: stay out of the dead angle, never up its right side at a light.

Day five, you. Zero point five promille — but zero point two in your first five licence years, so in practice zero. Drugs: any measurable amount. The yellow sticker means ask before driving; tired means stop. Licence, registration, [nl: WA] insurance, valid [nl: APK] — four boxes before any drive. The phone stays out of your hand, red lights included. Belts for all, a child seat below one metre thirty-five.

Day six, the kerb and the car. Stopping is not parking: the slash forbids parking only, the X forbids both. Five metres from junctions and zebras, never a driveway, never the cycle lane — no sign needed. The blue zone wants its disc, set to arrival rounded up. Rear fog only under fifty metres. One point six millimetres of tread, a load that cannot shift or hide a light, and on eco questions, the calm answer.

4. Practice — five insight-style questions.

The exam's own style now — situations, not rules. Pause, decide, then listen.

Question one. A ball bounces into the road from between parked cars, thirty metres ahead. The road itself is clear. What do you do?

Brake, immediately and firmly — treat it as a child already stepping out, because that is what it is about to be. Swerving around the ball while holding speed is the trap answer: it solves the ball, which was never the problem. The exam rewards the driver who reacts to the hidden hazard, not the visible one.

Question two. A bus has stopped at a bus stop ahead on your right. You can pass it easily. What is the insight answer?

Ease off and watch the front of the bus as you pass — a pedestrian who just got off may cross in front of it, invisible to you until the last step. "Pass at unchanged speed, it is only a stopped bus" fails the question. And from day four: if the bus signals to leave the stop while you approach, inside the built-up area you let it out.

Question three. Under the sign with the red X, a driver waits in the car, engine running, "just two minutes while my friend buys bread". Offence or not?

An offence. The X forbids stopping, and waiting in the car is exactly that — staying behind the wheel changes nothing, and neither does two minutes. Under the single slash it would have been different only for actually letting someone in or out or loading. Wording, again: the sign's verb is the question's answer.

Question four. Daytime, on the [nl: autosnelweg]. The car ahead does ninety and you want to overtake. May you briefly take one hundred and twenty to pass quickly?

No. The daytime limit is one hundred, and there is no overtaking exception to a speed limit — passing "quickly for safety" at one-twenty is simply speeding. If one hundred is not enough to overtake safely, the insight answer is: then you do not overtake. Limits cap the manoeuvre, not the other way round.

Question five. Outside the built-up area, dusk and drizzle, limit eighty. The question asks: is it wise to drive eighty here? A second question asks: may you drive eighty here?

Two different answers. You may — eighty is the limit and nothing forbids it. But it is not wise — dusk and rain cut what you can see and how you grip, and the standing duty is to match speed to conditions. If you answered both the same way, reread the verbs; the exam plants exactly this pair to catch drivers who answer the situation instead of the question.

Closing — exam day.

That is the course — seven days, the whole theory. Between now and Monday, the work is mock exams: full fifty-question practice runs, in English, until you score forty-seven or better twice in a row, reviewing every wrong answer until you can say why. The night before, stop — a rested head reads questions better than a crammed one. Monday morning: nine o'clock at Amsterdam Sloterdijk, Naritaweg one-fifty; bring your residence card or passport, and reservation number six-five-four-five checks you in. Fifty questions, forty-four to pass, and you have prepared for every one of them. Read the verb, back the careful answer, trust the ladder. Go get your theory certificate — [nl: veel succes].
